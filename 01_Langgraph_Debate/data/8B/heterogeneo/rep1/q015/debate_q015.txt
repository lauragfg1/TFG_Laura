[PNM] In distributed satellite constellations, orbital configuration plays a crucial role in balancing coverage and inter-satellite interference mitigation. The number of satellites, their distances, and orbital planes significantly impact the trade-offs between these factors.

Centralized topologies with synchronization ensure conflict-free communication using protocols like CDMA or hybrid TDMA/CDMA, offering high throughput and less delay. However, they may limit the number of satellites in a system due to the near-far problem and Multiple Access Interference (MAI).

On the other hand, distributed topologies employing LDMA (a hybrid of CSMA and TDMA) can achieve maximum channel utilization compared to pure CSMA and TDMA. However, they may not be optimal for large constellations due to time scheduling difficulties in a scalable network.

Power allocation and admission control are essential resource management techniques for interference suppression. The former ensures identical marginal return for every selected user, while the latter minimizes total accumulated delay from each user.

Inter-plane inter-satellite communication is crucial for constellations with multiple orbital planes. By identifying feasible satellite pairs and allocating orthogonal wireless resources, interference can be mitigated. However, it may not be possible for some satellite pairs due to their positions in different orbital planes.

In summary, the choice of protocols, topologies, and synchronization methods significantly impacts the performance of distributed satellite constellations. Optimizing constellation design requires considering the number of satellites, their distances, orbital planes, resource management techniques, and inter-plane communication capabilities to achieve optimal coverage and interference mitigation.
