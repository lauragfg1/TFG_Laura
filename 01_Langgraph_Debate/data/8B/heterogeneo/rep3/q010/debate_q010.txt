[MODERATOR] Good morning, everyone. Welcome to today's debate on interference mitigation techniques in multi-beam satellite systems. The challenge we're addressing is how these techniques adapt when handling tunneled versus non-tunneled traffic.

In a multi-beam system, multiple beams are used to cover different areas of the Earth, but this can lead to increased interference between beams and with terrestrial systems. Tunnelled traffic, which is routed through a satellite gateway before being transmitted to its final destination, poses unique challenges for interference mitigation techniques.

Dr. Patel, would you like to start by discussing your approach to handling tunneled versus non-tunneled traffic in multi-beam satellite systems?
------------------------------
[DLB] In multi-beam satellite systems, tunneled traffic requires additional processing at the gateway, increasing computational load and latency. This can degrade performance by up to 30% compared to non-tunneled traffic due to increased interference and resource contention. For instance, conventional network coding schemes (Phase IV) introduce phase shifts that must be carefully managed to avoid disrupting other signals, adding complexity to the design. Interference mitigation techniques like rate-splitting multiple access (RSMA) can enhance spectral efficiency but come with higher implementation costs in terms of hardware and software complexity.
------------------------------
[PNM] In multi-beam satellite systems, interference mitigation techniques are crucial to ensure efficient use of spectrum resources. Tunneled traffic, which passes through a gateway before transmission, poses unique challenges due to increased complexity and potential for increased interference.

One approach is to use Network Coding (NC) at the gateway to combine multiple data streams into a single merged stream. The satellite then broadcasts this merged stream to users, who can decode their intended signal using NC decoders. This method reduces interference by minimizing the number of simultaneous transmissions and improving spectral efficiency.

However, NC introduces additional complexity in terms of feed weight design and potential increased latency due to the need for decoding at each user. It also requires precise Channel State Information (CSI) at both the transmitter and receiver to ensure effective decoding.

In contrast, non-tunneled traffic does not pass through a gateway, reducing complexity but potentially increasing interference due to multiple simultaneous transmissions. In this case, traditional techniques such as orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) or code division multiple access (CDMA) can be used to mitigate interference by assigning unique frequencies or codes to each user.

Overall, the choice between tunneled and non-tunneled traffic depends on the specific system requirements, tradeoffs between complexity and interference, and availability of CSI.
