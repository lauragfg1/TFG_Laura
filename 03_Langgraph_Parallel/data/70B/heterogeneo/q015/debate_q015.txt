[DLB] In distributed satellite constellations, the choice between Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and Geostationary Orbit (GEO) involves critical trade-offs influenced by satellite altitude. LEO, at 160-2,000 km, offers lower latency due to shorter signal travel distances but faces challenges like atmospheric interference and ionospheric delays. These factors can attenuate signals and introduce delays, necessitating more robust modulation and error correction. Additionally, LEO constellations require more satellites for coverage, increasing potential interference and complexity in management.

GEO, at 35,786 km, experiences higher latency due to longer signal paths but benefits from being above most atmospheric interference, resulting in less attenuation. However, ionospheric effects are less pronounced at this altitude. GEO requires fewer satellites, simplifying interference management but reducing capacity and making it less suitable for real-time applications.

Thus, LEO offers higher capacity and lower latency at the cost of increased complexity and resource-intensive interference management, while GEO provides simpler management with higher latency and lower capacity. The choice hinges on balancing performance, latency, and resource allocation needs.
