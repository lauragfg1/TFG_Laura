[DLB] GEO satellites offer large coverage but high latency, impacting real-time monitoring in rural substations. LEO satellites reduce delay by being closer to Earth, but require more frequent re-pointing, increasing complexity and potential interference. The closer spacing of LEO satellites also enhances array factor effects, potentially boosting capacity at the cost of increased beam management overhead. Budget-wise, LEO networks may offer better performance/cost trade-offs for critical applications like substation monitoring despite higher initial deployment costs.

[PNM] In hybrid networks for rural substation monitoring, GEO satellites offer large stationary footprints but suffer from high latency due to their geostationary orbits (>550ms). This delay can lead to increased response times and reduced real-time control capabilities. On the other hand, LEO satellites have lower delays, making them suitable for applications requiring lower latency, such as remote monitoring. However, LEO satellites require pointing towards the satellite, which may be challenging in mobile or rapidly changing environments. Additionally, LEO constellations can offer global coverage but may suffer from interference due to their large number of satellites and close proximity to each other. To mitigate this, routing algorithms like bandwidth delay satellite routing can be used, which prioritize satellites based on both bandwidth and delay. Overall, the choice between GEO and LEO orbits depends on the specific requirements of the application, such as latency tolerance, coverage area, and mobility.

[PNM] **Hybrid Networks with Orbit Diversity**

In hybrid networks for rural substation monitoring, a combination of GEO and LEO orbits can offer improved latency-performance trade-offs while maintaining global coverage. The LEO satellites, with their lower delay, provide high-speed data transfer between substations, reducing round-trip delays below 550ms. A single GEO satellite can be used for backhaul connectivity to the central monitoring station, offering large stationary footprints and ensuring global coverage. However, this approach requires careful resource allocation to manage the differences in delay and bandwidth between the two orbits.

Bandwidth Delay Satellite Routing (BDSR) can be employed as a routing algorithm, prioritizing LEO satellites for lower-latency communication while considering their limited bandwidth. Additionally, LDMA (a hybrid of CSMA and TDMA) can help achieve maximum channel utilization in the hybrid network, addressing the potential contention issues arising from multiple satellites.

By combining the strengths of both GEO and LEO orbits, this hybrid approach can offer a more robust solution for rural substation monitoring applications, balancing latency, capacity, and global coverage requirements.
