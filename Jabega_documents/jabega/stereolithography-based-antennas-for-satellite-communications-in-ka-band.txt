stereolithography based antennas for satellite communications in ka band

by joana s. silva, maría garcía vigueras, tomislav debogovic´, jorge r. costa, senior member ieee, carlos a. fernandes, senior member ieee, and juan r. mosig, fellow ieee

abstract | this paper illustrates the role of additive manufacuring (am) as enabling technology to realize high performance low cost antennas for ka band applications. in addition to the inherent electromagnetic challenges implicit in the conception of such complex devices, this paper also points out the strinent limitations that appear when opting for classical fabricaion techniques, based on assembled split block models. am emerges in this context as a change of paradigm, allowing monoithic fabrication and design freedom, which result in substantial improvements in terms of compactness, mass, simplicity, cost, and production time. two different antennas for ka band satelite communications are presented here, namely a wideband horn and a dual band circular cavity. both prototypes are fabriated using a stereolithographic (sla) am process followed by metal coating. this fabrication approach is especially well suited to the implementation of these designs, since they have internal shapes that are inaccessible to conventional machining tools. the experimental results are not only in very good agreement with the theoretical predictions but also demonstrate improvements over the performances achieved by traditional milling and assemly fabrication approaches, thereby confirming the validity and great potential of sla for ka band satellite communications.

i. i n t roduc t ion

the ever growing demand for high speed data links together with the increasing congestion in the traditional microwave spectrum are pushing for the exploitation of higher microave and millimeter wave (mm wave) frequencies, includng the so called ka , q , and v bands. associated to this movement toward higher frequencies comes the enhanceent of technological constraints imposed on electronic equipment for satellite communications (satcom), mainly concerning high versatility and performance, compactness, and both reduced cost and weight. consequently, the impleentation of modern antenna systems requires, simultaneusly, to adopt new electromagnetic (em) design strategies and to use innovative manufacturing techniques. both these demands are successfully tackled in this paper when targetng specifically at ka band antennas.

officially allocated to the spectrum between 26.5 and 40 ghz, the ka band commonly includes also part of the k band around 20 ghz. the identified intervals for both downlink (rx) and uplink (tx) frequency bands are, respecively, 17.7 21.2 ghz (civil: 17.7 20.2 ghz; military: 20.2 21.2 ghz) and 27.5 31 ghz (civil: 27.5 30 ghz; military: 30 31 ghz) , . the adoption of this band has brought an upheaval in the satcom industry comparable to the migration that took place from lnd c bands to ku band in the 1980s and 1990s . yet, the impact of the present expansion seems to be more significant due to ka band high versatility that has implied deep architectural changes. one of them is related to the beam coverage: while regional beams covering large areas of earth's surface are the dominant architecture in cnd ku satellites, ka satellites mostly provide spot beam covrage allowing frequency reuse operation . this fact leads to the common association of ka band with high throughput satellites and corresponding broadband services. though the spot beam approach was first exploited in c band and then in ku band , benefits are higher in ka band, allowng ka satellites to provide an impressive reduction in their footprint spot beam, thus providing a significant increase in channel capacity. furthermore, comparable size antenas provide higher gain at ka band than at lower frequenies, which means that similar services can be provided from smaller terminals .

satcom radiating systems operating at high frequenies (typically, waveguide based antennas and associated feed chain components) have been traditionally built using subtractive fabrication methods (such as milling, turning, spark eroding, molding, or shaping by plastic deforation). these approaches start to present significant limitations to comply with the current challenging requirements. difficulties stem mainly from the existnce of very small and virtually inaccessible internal parts in the device that need to be very precisely machined. in order to gain access to these internal channels, the clasically adopted approach relies on splitting the piece into several sub blocks, which are later assembled using screws and alignment pins. though good precision has proven achievable up to mm wave frequencies , this solution results in bulky devices and poses several drawbacks concerning performance uncertainty, high prouction costs, long lead times, need for complex assembly procedures, and nonnegligible design limitations, due to manufacturing constraints .

additive manufacturing (am) techniques , usully known as 3 d printing, have already radically changed the way to realize everyday life objects and seem a promising way to explore when conceiving modern satcom systems . am is considered by the european framework programme horizon 2020 as one of the six key enabling technologies (kets) future drivers of innovation in europe . its industrial applications are increasing exponentially, and the number of funded am related projects has expeienced a growth of 136% since 2014 . when properly implemented, am may become an advantageous alternaive, offering the following valuable advantages to simplify and improve the fabrication of antenna subsystems. to start with, am allows envisioning monolithic complex designs with original tridimensional shapes that could hardly be implemented using conventional techniques. in addition to this new design freedom, shortcomings related to the aforeentioned split block approach are inherently solved, or at least alleviated, thus achieving valuable reduction in size, weight, and cost.

during the last years, the panorama of am techniques has been drastically evolving, being capable today to disinguish between more than ten different types. currently, the most exploited and commonly used approach for space oriented metallic devices is selective laser sintering (sls) , , with aluminum powder as the building material. recent successful realizations have confirmed sls advantages in terms of design freedom and volume reduction when conceiving radio frequency (rf) components operatng at low microwave frequencies , . unfortunately, at higher frequencies, these pluses are offset by large mechanial tolerances (around 250 μ m ). also, surface roughness (50 μ​m compared with the 20 μ​m achievable by milling ) leads to significant losses at ka band and above . though postfabrication procedures can be applied in order to improve surface properties (such as sandblasting or electrolytic silver plating), this step results in higher costs and usually preludes monolithic fabrication. direct metal laser sintering (dmls) has recently appeared as an improved version of sls (with surface roughness below 10 μ​m ); promising high precision waveguide antenna arrays have already been reported . turning to alternative solutions, stereolithogaphy (sla), based on nonmetallic polymer 3 d printing, appears in this context as a promising technique , . in contrast to previous techniques, sla allows at the same time: fine surface finish (1 μ​m or lower), high manufacturng precision (25 μ​m), and mass savings (thanks to the employment of light polymers as the building material ). on the other hand, to achieve sought after electrical functionalities, sla can be combined with a metallization procedure. a high quality chemical procedure that allows homogeneous metallic coating has been recently reported; it provides low levels of surface roughness and, thus, enables realization of rf components and antennas from ku band up to 300 ghz . this technique ensures high perforance even for highly convoluted geometrical structures, such as the w band corrugated horn from and .

the present contribution is based on sla comined with the previously mentioned metallization techique for the implementation of two different ka band radiators. the results that are here presented arise from public domain research and projects involving parters from école polytechnique fédérale de lausanne (lausanne, switzerland), institut d'électronique et de télécommunications de rennes (rennes, france), instituto de telecomunicações (lisbon, portugal), as well as the swiss company swissto12 (lausanne, switzerland), the european space agency in the netherlands, and fundação para a ciência e a tecnologia (lisbon, portugal) , , .

this paper is organized as follows. section ii provides a detailed description of the sla procedure followed for the antennas implementation, together with a brief review on the considered ka band requirements. section iii describes the first conceived antenna, which is based on a quad ridged horn. note that here we review the design that was origially presented by some of the authors in , while making special emphasis on the manufacturing process and includng additional unpublished results. the first prototype is realized employing classical milling, exhibiting measured performance far from the expected theoretical values. a secnd prototype is then 3 d printed basing on sla, giving rise to a much more compact and reliable device. a clear comarison of both prototypes is carried out, highlighting the am improvements in terms of ease of fabrication, perforance enhancement, and mass savings. section iv presents a fully original design that performs similarly to the previous one, though being much more compact. the geometry of this new device is particularly suitable for sla, and based on a slotted cavity. a comparison between theoretical and measured results validates again both the original design concept and the sla fabrication process. finally, concluions are drawn in section v.

ka band antennas have become a key research topic whose interest is strongly linked to the boost of satellite on theove (sotm) applications, toward the desired goal of being always connected . these applications allow ground terinals to be placed on moving platforms, such as high speed trains or small boats. besides the need to cope with the wide frequency separation between rx and tx bands, antennas should radiate with circular polarization (cp), thus removng the need of polarization alignment . additionally, sotm requires high gain beam steerable antenna systems in order to keep up the link with the satellite. mechanical beam steering solutions are usually based on the combinaion of a primary antenna (also known as feeder) that satsfies the polarization and bandwidth requirements, with director devices able to enhance the system gain (such as, for example, lenses, reflectors, reflectarrays, transmitarays, and fabry perot resonators) . in this contriution, we concentrate specifically on the development of those primary feed antennas. several potential candidates can already be found in the literature; they are based either on printed circuit technology (pcb) , waveguide structures , , or on a hybrid combination of both these approaches . when aiming at robust perforance and reduced losses, hollow waveguide designs are normally preferred. however, these solutions are commonly bulky, heavy, and expensive. as explained in section i, sla seems to alleviate these constraints, as it allows to impleent compact waveguide based designs with low weight (printed out of light polymers) and at low cost. in addition to compactness, sla capability to provide monolithic pieces avoids most assembling steps required by traditional subractive techniques, thus removing as well the need for extra external material. all these features, together with its assoiated high design freedom and short lead times, make sla very attractive for the conception of ka band feeds.

the 3 d printed prototypes presented in this paper have been produced by swissto12, which is a company specialized in sla. its manufacturing process is presented in the remainer of this section. the first fabrication step is the 3 d printing of the device body. this is done through a polymerization of a liquid photopolymer using a laser. the laser solidifies layer by layer the liquid material on top of a build platform that, at the same time, is being lowered. in this way, local precision of ± 25 μ​m can be achieved, with a maximum tolerance of ±​ 200 μ​m on large dimensions (current setup is able to produce devices with volumes of approximately 300 × 300 ×​ 350 mm ). these values are likely to improve further as the printing techology is evolving. after printing and cleaning the polymer body, there is a preparation step, where the polymer surfaces are treated by chemical products in order to improve the adhesion of the subsequent metal coating. afterwards, a uniorm metallization of the entire device, including their comlex inner shapes, is done through a chemical electroless proess that results in a surface roughness below 1 μ​m. the metal used is typically copper, and the targeted metal thickness is 7 10 skin depths. additionally, a very thin (0.1 μ​m) gold layer can be added for passivation. for this ka band application, the metal layer deposited on the polymer body is more than 3 μ​m thick, which is enough to ensure full rf conductivity since the skin depth of copper at ka band roughly ranges between 0.4 and 0.5 μ​m. the basic test to validate the quality of metalization is an adhesion test where one attempts to peal the metal off by using a scotch like tape (astm standard d33599). another test consists of thermal cycling where the device is subjected to several cycles between extreme temperaures (e.g., −55°c to 75°c for airborne standard eurocae edb14g). this test can be challenging since the polymer and metal have different coefficients of thermal expansion which puts the coating continuity at risk. yet, the tests are passed successfully thanks to the choice of polymer materials and the developed plating process.

iii. q ua d r i d ged hor n a n t e n na

the first device presented in this paper is a wideband horn that was originally presented in . the antenna radiates with cp, and it is able to operate simultaneously at both rx and tx sub ka bands ranging from 19.7 to 20.2 ghz and 29.5 to 30 ghz. this horn is intended to feed a dielectric lens, constituting a low cost sotm ground terminal . in the following subsections, we first introduce the horn operation mechanism and then we present two manufactured prototypes, one based on computer numerical control (cnc) milling and a second one based on sla. the performance of both antennas is discussed, highlighting the advantages proided by am. finally, we show for the first time the operaion of this antenna in a sotm system based on the simple mechanical beam steering approach introduced in where a dielectric lens is rotated and tilted over the feeder.

a. antenna configuration

the geometry of the complete device is presented in . it is composed of a quad ridged conical horn antenna fed by a dedicated coaxial to waveguide transition; both designed using a commercial electromagnetic solver . preliminary studies guided the authors toward the adoption of a 45° slant horn aperture as depicted in fig. 1. this was the result of a specific optimization required to achieve a maximum beam tilt elevation angle, while assurng a good compromise between gain, cross polarization, and phase center stability. then, to achieve full beam scaning in azimuth, the 45° slant aperture should be rotatable with respect to the body of the horn. moreover, the horn design required internal metallic tapered ridges [fig. 1(b)] to achieve the needed frequency bandwidth. in this design, the horn must have four symmetric ridges to achieve cp. in addition, a truly complete cp structure would also need a second coaxial connector to implement the required dual classical excitations, namely, equal amplitude and 90° phase shift. however, and for the sake of simplicity, it was decided to conceive a linearly polarized (lp) launcher and measure the radiation in two orthogonal lp by rotatng the launcher 90° with respect to the horn. thereupon, the final expected cp behavior can be obtained by postrocessing. further details about the design are reported in and .

the overall length of the horn is 61.2 mm which correponds to 4 . 08λ at 20 ghz. for the whole structure where the transition is added to the horn, this figure increases to 109.3 mm, which is equivalent to 7 . 29λ at 20 ghz. this structure cannot be considered a compact device, but here the main goal was a proof of concept, demonstrating the possibility of achieving similar beam steering performances at both rx and tx ka bands with a very simple mechanical setup, where the rf head and feed are fixed, thus avoiding expensive mm wave rotary joints. b. prototype discussion

to simplify an already complex design, the possibility of rotating the 45° slant aperture was not considered. the main goal was to measure the performance in elevation since due to the symmetries the elevation radiation pattern is expected to be independent of the rotation of the aperture and can be measured in a single azimuthal angle.

the first attempt of prototype manufacture was done using a cnc milling machine with a precision of 50 μ m as is presented in fig. 2(a). the biggest drawback associted to the use of this technique is illustrated in fig. 2(b). it was not possible to fabricate the antenna in a single piece and then, the first important step was to conceive an equivalent split block model. as detailed in , the complete prototype model requires nine different pieces in order to enable for cp (allowing the 90° rotation of the launcher), imposing an arduous assembling process with a considerable number of screws and alignment pins. even if the design is limited to single lp, it requires five differnt pieces [an example shown in fig. 2(b)]. this process is normally responsible for air gaps and misalignments between the pieces, which undermines the benefit of the original machine accuracy.

as reported in section iii c, the return loss of the cnc prototype did not agree well with the simulations. therefore, a second prototype was manufactured using the previously mentioned sla technique as is presented in fig. 2(c). the same prototype before metallization is presented in fig. 2(d) in order to show the monolithic piece where alignment pins and screws are not necessary. since in the cnc prototype, cp was obtained by postprocessing two lp measurements, it was decided that for the sla prototype only an lp polarizaion was necessary for comparison. therefore, a monolithic sla lp prototype was built, integrating both the horn and the launcher.

by observing both figs. 1 and 2, the similarities between the computer aided design (cad) model and the sla prootype are evident. this fact alone is already an advantage, as it smooths the hard and time consuming intermediate planning steps between the design and fabrication phases. moreover, as also clearly shown in fig. 2, the sla techique allowed considerable savings of the external material that led to a dramatic reduction of weight in comparison to the cnc milling horn antenna system: from 484 to 28.5 g, being a 17 fold reduction.

a. principle of operation

with its fourfold symmetry and four accesses, this antenna is ready for producing cp through sequential rotaion. this is realized by feeding the antenna at four ports through coaxial cables inserted from the bottom of the cavty up to its top cover (fig. 8). the bfn itself, comprising two microstrip circuits sharing the same ground plane (not shown in fig. 9 for the sake of simplicity), is designed to provide equal magnitudes and successive 90° phase shift to each coaxial cable. note that each circuit is associated to one sub ka band (rx/tx) which allows simultaneous operaion in right hand cp (rhcp) in rx band and left hand cp (lhcp) in tx band. further details about the bfn are reported in . even with this bfn, the total length of the system is 70 mm ( 4 . 66λ at 20 ghz). this is a 36% reducion in length respect to the entire feed system of section ii composed of the horn and the transition.

because of the simultaneous excitation of the four antenna ports, an active input reflection coefficient must be considered for performance assessment. under a premise of the ideal 90° phase shift, the active input reflection coeffiient at port 1 can be formulated as active s 11 = s 11 +​ j s 21 − s 31 − j s 41 = s 11 − s 31 . owing to the structure symetry, the active input reflection coefficients of all the ports should be equal.

the geometrical dimensions of the antenna are optiized in order to ensure operation in both civil and military rx and tx bands. the success of the optimization is conirmed in fig. 10 which shows the active input reflection coefficient to be below −10 db within both entire rx and tx bands. the antenna presents fractional bandwidths of 22.8% and 12.6% in rx and tx bands, respectively. besides, the antenna also shows an interesting em behavior in the middle of both bands, acting almost as a notch filter for supression of undesired signals. owing to the complexity of the structure and its small dimensions, the authors opted here directly for 3 d printng to realize the prototype. as aforementioned, sla accuacy is in the order of 25 μ m. in such a compact device, where a great accuracy is critical, the precision of sla is deemed adequate to opt for this technology. again, am enales monolithic fabrication, while the use of cnc milling techniques (although potentially even more precise) would preclude a single piece prototype and eventually introduce assembling errors. photographs of the final sla prototype are shown in fig. 11. as can be observed, this is a monolithic device devoid of screws and alignment pins, and built out of lightweight nonmetallic polymer, as customary in sla. the weight of this element is 0.5 g, which is a remarkable achievement for such an antenna element, and encourages its further miniaturization to build an array. the production of such a cost effective panel of elements would be espeially appealing when applied to leo mega constellations. the authors are currently exploring the conception of such a device, where the bfn functions are common to the whole panel, and thus, only implemented once. still, it should be highlighted here that, though the reduction of the element diameter is feasible, the conception of a "3 d printable" bfn remains a challenge.

this paper demonstrates the strong potential of am sla technology for the realization of ka band antennas. we preent two 3 d printed designs and prove their validity experientally. the first design is a wideband quad ridged horn, while the second one is based on a dual band slotted cavity. both antennas can serve as feeds for sotm applications at ka band. we show an example of the use of the horn when illuminating a dielectric lens.

the presented antennas clearly illustrate the crucial role played by am in the realization of devices operatng at ka band. its benefits with respect to traditional machining techniques are discussed, and stem mainly from sla possibility of producing light monolithic devices that are 3 d printed out of light polymers and metalized afterward. these characteristics contribte not only to reducing the weight and the cost of the terminal, but also to increasing design flexibility. therefore, this preliminary study highlights sla strong potential for satcom applications, and pushes for its  further assessment.
